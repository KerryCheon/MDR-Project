Based on the repository's evaluation reports (like the `derived_8.1_pos-eval` series) and the gating documentation (`docs/gating.md`), here is exactly why the oracle model performs so well, why the global model struggles, and how the team has been making specialists more resilient to misrouted data.

### 1. Why does the Single Global Model perform worse?
Soil moisture dynamics exhibit highly non-linear, regime-dependent behavior driven by completely different physical processes:
*   **Dry Regime:** Low-variance, slow-drying, and heavily dominated by static soil-texture features.
*   **Wet Regime:** High-variance, rapid gravitational drainage, heavily driven by rainfall events and saturation peaks.

A **Single Global Model** struggles because it is forced to learn a single function that compromises across these contradictory physical behaviors. This leads to smoothing at the extremes, failing at the distribution tails, and poor out-of-station spatial generalization.

### 2. Why does the Oracle 3-Regime Model perform better?
The **Oracle Model** uses ground-truth labels to perfectly route each sample to a dedicated specialist. 
Because the specialists only ever see their specific slice of the data, they don't have to compromise. The wet specialist can aggressively map rainfall to moisture spikes without ruining the dry specialist's ability to model slow, low-variance evaporation. This lack of compromise allows the oracle MoE to hit an impressive $R^2$ of ~`0.87` (compared to the global model's ~`0.48` on the WA spatial splits).

### 3. Making Specialists Resilient to Misrouted Data
Your intuition is spot on: real-world MoE routing is imperfect, and when hard-gating is used, a slight routing error (e.g., predicting `0.199` instead of `0.201`) routes the sample to the completely wrong specialist, introducing massive step-function errors (the "routing penalty").

The project has actually explored several strategies to make specialists and the overall MoE more resilient to this exact problem:

*   **Adapted Training (The most effective for hard gating):** In the recent `pos-eval-2.0` experiment, the specialists were trained **directly on the imperfectly routed data** generated by the router, rather than the clean, ground-truth regimes. By exposing the specialists to the actual misclassification noise they will see at inference time, they co-adapt to those specific errors. This single change allowed the imperfect 3-Regime MoE to jump from an $R^2$ of `0.09` to `0.49`, finally beating the global model.
*   **Reducing to 2-Regimes:** The transition zone is highly heterogeneous and extremely difficult for a router to classify (only 58% routing accuracy for 3 regimes). Collapsing the problem into a binary 2-Regime router (Dry vs. Wet/Transition) boosted routing accuracy to 83%, reducing the amount of misrouted data the specialists had to deal with and lifting the MoE performance to $R^2$ = `0.52`.
*   **Soft Gating (Weighted Blending):** Instead of making a hard routing choice, earlier models (like `v20.2`) used sigmoid functions centered around the boundaries to smoothly blend the predictions of multiple specialists, which heavily mitigates step-function boundary errors.
*   **Base-Dominant Confidence Ensembles:** In `v21.4`, the router was designed to measure "confidence" (distance from the threshold boundary). If a sample fell near a boundary where routing is highly uncertain, the gating mechanism would dynamically fall back to the Single Global Model's prediction, using the specialists only when confidence was high. 

If you are looking to build a new MoE iteration, the combination of **2-Regime Routing** coupled with **Adapted Training** seems to be the most successful structural approach for dealing with imperfect routers so far.

Can't a single more internally learn multiple functions?

---

Yes, in theory, a single model with enough capacity (like a deep Neural Network or a massive XGBoost ensemble) *should* be able to learn multiple functions by internally partitioning the feature space. Decision trees, by definition, split the data into different regions and fit different values to them.

However, in practice, a single global model struggles to do this effectively for this specific soil moisture problem due to a few key ML bottlenecks:

### 1. The Loss Function is Dominated by the Wet Regime
A single global model trains using a single objective function (like minimizing overall RMSE or Absolute Error). 
Because the **Wet Regime** has massive variance (huge spikes from rainfall) and the **Dry Regime** has very low variance (slow evaporation), the absolute errors in the wet regime are much larger. The global loss function gets dominated by the wet regime's gradients. The model ends up dedicating most of its learning capacity (tree splits or network weights) to fixing large wet errors, essentially ignoring the dry regime's nuanced, small-scale dynamics. 

### 2. Feature Competition 
Different regimes rely on completely different features. The dry regime is heavily dependent on static soil texture and long-term temperature, while the wet regime is driven almost entirely by short-term precipitation and recent satellite readings. 
In a single XGBoost model, these features have to compete for the critical early splits in the trees. If the model splits on precipitation first, it fragments the data too much before it can properly utilize the soil texture features needed for the dry regime. 

### 3. Smoothing and "Regression to the Mean"
Because a single model is trying to minimize the expected error across all data points, it tends to learn a "compromise" function at the boundaries. If a sample is borderline, the safest mathematical bet for the global model to minimize RMSE is to guess the average between the dry behavior and the wet behavior. This results in the model severely under-predicting peaks and over-predicting valleys (smoothing the extremes).

### 4. Generalization to Unseen Stations
When an XGBoost model internally learns to separate regimes, it often overfits to the specific numerical thresholds of the stations it trained on (e.g., learning that `LST_modis > 35` means "dry"). When you transfer that model to a new station in Washington state where the baseline temperatures are different, those hard-coded internal splits break. 

### The MoE Advantage
By explicitly separating the routing task from the prediction task, you decouple these competing objectives:
1.  **The Router** gets to dedicate 100% of its capacity just to learning the feature relationships that indicate *which regime we are in*, without worrying about predicting the exact moisture value.
2.  **The Specialists** get to use a loss function that is perfectly scaled to their specific variance, and their trees don't have to waste early splits deciding if it's raining or not—they already know.
